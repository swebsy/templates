For parents of 13- to 24-year-olds

Not sure if it’s a phase or something more?

You don’t have to be sure before you call. Twenty minutes on the phone is usually enough to tell whether I’m the right kind of help, and if I’m not, who is.

Book a free call ↓

Is this the right kind of help?

What I see most

  • Anxiety and panic, including before school or sport.
  • Not wanting to go to school.
  • Low mood, or a kind of flatness.
  • Friendship trouble, and fights at home.
  • Questions about identity, and coming out.
  • A first year at college or work that isn’t going well.

Who I refer on, with names

  • Children under 13.
  • Eating disorders that need a medical team.
  • Custody and court evaluations.
  • Couples.
  • Crisis care, which needs more than one appointment a week.

I don’t prescribe. When medication comes up, I work with your family doctor or a psychiatrist.

How soon?

Today.

If they talk about not wanting to be alive, have hurt themselves, or you think they’re in danger: call or text 988, or go to the nearest emergency room. Call 911 if it’s happening now.

I’m not a crisis service, and messages can wait a day for an answer.

This week is fine.

If something has changed for a few weeks: sleep, school, friends, mood, how much they’re eating or talking. You don’t need a reason that sounds serious enough. Book the free call and describe what you’re seeing.

“Will I be shut out?”

No. You’ll know less than you might like, and more than you might fear.

You’ll hear

Whether they’re coming, how it’s going in general, and the goals you agreed together. A 15-minute call every six to eight weeks; they know, and can join.

You won’t hear

What they say in the room. That privacy is what makes them talk at all.

I’ll tell you without asking

If they or someone else is at serious risk. Suspected abuse goes to the authorities, and a court order can require records. When it’s safe and possible, I talk with them first.

If your teen started on their own (Maryland allows it from 12), or is 18 or over, you hear only what they sign for.

How to bring it up.

  1. Say what you’ve noticed, not what you think it means. “You’ve seemed tired of school” lands better than “I think you’re depressed.”
  2. Offer the free call as something they can be on, or not. Many teens sit in on the second half.
  3. Send them the page written for them. It’s short, and nobody is quizzed on it.
